Pose estimation

How a computer turns video into a skeleton

What a camera can and cannot observe

Two words matter throughout this tutorial. Kinematics describes motion you can see: where the joints are and how they move. Dynamics describes what causes motion: muscle effort, loads, and the forces between the body and the ground. A camera captures the first. The research here asks whether the second can be inferred from it [1].

Human pose estimation

Pose estimation software looks at each video frame, finds the body's joints, and links them into a skeleton. Deep neural networks took over this task from older methods, and later work extended it from flat 2D points to 3D skeletons. All of these methods learn joint positions and angles. None of them says anything about external forces or balance [1].

OpenPose and the Body-25 skeleton

PressNET's authors use OpenPose, which finds joints with Part Affinity Fields, a way of learning which joints belong together. Its Body-25 model reports 25 keypoints per person, and each keypoint comes with an x position, a y position, and a confidence score. Besides the usual shoulders, elbows, hips, knees, and ankles, it includes the heels and toes of both feet, which matters when the goal is foot pressure [1].

Detection is not perfect. In the authors' videos the eye and ear keypoints had zero confidence in more than half the frames, because the head was turned or hidden [1].

From skeleton to network input

Before the joints reach the network, they are shifted so the hip is the origin. That removes the effect of where the person stood in the frame. The confidence values are dropped, and the hip itself is removed since it is now always zero. That leaves 24 joints with 2 coordinates each, or 48 numbers per frame [1].
