Future and conclusions

Where this line of research is headed, and what it adds up to

Future directions from the research

The original authors describe pose-to-pressure as one step toward a broader goal: recovering muscle activation, weight distribution, and other forces from video alone, without any wearable sensor. They frame this as a new sub-field sitting between plain pose estimation and full biomechanical analysis [1]. The follow-up work continues in that direction by adding base of support estimation on top of pressure and center of pressure [2]. Both papers point to uses in kinesiology, medicine, and robotics, alongside sports [1][2].

Possible sports applications

These extend the research past what it has actually shown, since neither paper tested athletes or fast movement.

  • Balance and stability tracking for sports where footing decides performance, like skating or gymnastics.
  • Foot-placement analysis for running, cutting, or jumping mechanics.
  • Lower-cost injury prevention or rehabilitation monitoring, without needing a pressure mat or motion capture lab.
  • Feedback for coaches, if pose-to-pressure can eventually run fast enough for near-real-time use.

Conclusions

A single video frame gives a skeleton, and a skeleton is kinematics: it shows where the body is, not what forces act on it. PressNET showed that a neural network can take that skeleton and predict foot pressure well enough to also recover a reasonably accurate center of pressure, beating a nearest-neighbor baseline by a wide margin [1]. The follow-up study confirmed the idea holds on more data and pushed it further, into base of support [2]. Both papers were tested only on a small number of people doing one slow, controlled activity indoors, so the biggest open question is whether the same approach holds up for fast, varied, real-world athletic movement.
